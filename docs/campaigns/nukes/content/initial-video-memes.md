# [DRAFT] Initial Video & Meme Content: Survivor Anecdotes

This brief defines the first short-form content series for the **We Don't Need Nukes** campaign. The series uses imagined survivor anecdotes to make nuclear risk feel personal, ordinary, and preventable rather than abstract or spectacular.

## 1. Core Concept

### Setting

The frame is a post-apocalyptic world, approximately a decade after a significant nuclear event. The event is already part of history. The audience meets survivors who are looking back on the ordinary decisions, assumptions, delays, and misunderstandings that made the disaster possible.

The setting should feel lived-in rather than cinematic: repaired clothing, improvised infrastructure, faded public notices, empty civic buildings, hand-maintained radios, and familiar objects that have outlasted the systems around them.

### Point of View

Each meme or video is an imagined/generated anecdote from one survivor. They speak as a normal person with 20/20 hindsight, not as a soldier, scientist, politician, or omniscient narrator. They remember a specific moment and explain what they misunderstood at the time.

The emotional premise is:

> “It did not feel like the end of the world when it started. That is what we should have paid attention to.”

The survivor is not presenting a complete historical account. They are telling a small story that reveals a larger failure: complacency, escalation, false confidence, broken communication, or the belief that someone else would stop it.

## 2. Creative Principles

1. **Start with the mundane.** A school pickup, a delayed train, a customer-service call, a weather alert, or a family group chat should be the doorway into the story.
2. **Make the hindsight specific.** The speaker should identify one detail they now understand differently.
3. **Keep the narrator human.** They can be funny, embarrassed, defensive, tired, or uncertain. They do not need perfect language or a polished political analysis.
4. **Imply the catastrophe; do not spectacle-ize it.** The series is about preventable choices and their consequences, not impressive explosions.
5. **Leave room for recognition.** The viewer should be able to think, “I have heard someone say that,” before thinking, “That could happen here.”
6. **End in the present tense.** The final beat should connect the anecdote to the campaign’s premise: we do not need systems that can end civilization by mistake, miscalculation, or design.

## 3. Repeatable Short-Form Structure

### Video format: 30–60 seconds

1. **Cold open (0–5 seconds):** A plainspoken line that sounds like a personal memory.
   - “The last normal thing I did was complain about the parking app.”
   - “We thought the alert was another test.”
2. **Specific anecdote (5–30 seconds):** One person, one place, one small sequence of events.
3. **Hindsight turn (30–45 seconds):** The narrator names the assumption or failure they now recognize.
4. **Quiet closing line (45–60 seconds):** A restrained campaign connection.
   - “Nobody needs a perfect plan for the end of the world. We need fewer ways to start it.”
   - “That was when I learned that ‘probably fine’ is not a safety system.”

### Static meme format

- **Frame 1:** A recognizable everyday situation.
- **Frame 2:** The survivor’s hindsight detail.
- **Frame 3:** A short, non-preachy connection to the campaign.

Keep text large enough for mobile viewing, use one idea per asset, and avoid dense explanatory captions. The visual language can be slightly worn or archival, but it should remain legible and shareable.

## 4. Initial Story/Meme Set

These are fictional premises for the first batch. They should be clearly labeled as imagined stories in the content metadata or caption, without interrupting the emotional opening of the asset.

### 1. “The Test Alert”

A municipal employee remembers dismissing an emergency alert because the city had run so many drills. Their hindsight is not that they were careless; it is that a system trained everyone to ignore the difference between practice and reality.

**Meme close:** “If the warning sounds like every other warning, people stop hearing it.”

### 2. “The Group Chat”

A parent recalls the family chat filling with screenshots, jokes, and contradictory claims. Everyone waited for a trusted person to confirm what was happening, while every trusted person waited for official confirmation.

**Meme close:** “We had six ways to share a rumor and no way to share certainty.”

### 3. “The Night Shift”

A hospital cleaner describes the moment the night staff realized the building’s emergency procedures assumed a short outage, not a world where supply chains and communications had failed together.

**Meme close:** “The plan was built for an interruption. Nobody planned for the plan to be gone.”

### 4. “The Weather Report”

A local broadcaster remembers reading an ordinary forecast while producers quietly replaced the rundown behind them. The audience could see that something was wrong before anyone was authorized to say it.

**Meme close:** “We thought calm meant safe. Sometimes it only means nobody has permission to speak.”

### 5. “The Meeting That Ran Late”

A junior civil servant remembers a meeting that kept getting extended because no one wanted to be the first person to say that the assumptions had changed. The crisis did not require a villain; it required enough people to defer the next decision.

**Meme close:** “The most dangerous sentence in the room was: ‘Let’s wait for one more update.’”

### 6. “The Full Battery”

A teenager remembers being proud that their phone was fully charged. Ten years later, they understand that information without functioning institutions, transport, and trust could not get their family to safety.

**Meme close:** “Preparedness is more than having one useful thing.”

### 7. “The Comment Section”

A survivor recalls arguing online about whether the threat was exaggerated. Their final memory of the old world is refreshing the page for someone else’s certainty instead of asking what they could do to reduce the risk.

**Meme close:** “We treated uncertainty like permission to do nothing.”

### 8. “The Photograph”

A person shows a faded picture of a neighborhood celebration and points out the one person who left early because they had an uneasy feeling. The story does not claim intuition could have saved everyone; it asks why society expects ordinary people to sense dangers designed to be irreversible.

**Meme close:** “We should not need a lucky feeling to survive a preventable threat.”

## 5. Tone and Visual Direction

- **Voice:** conversational, restrained, observant, occasionally dry; never like a campaign speech.
- **Performance:** direct-to-camera testimony, voiceover over still images, or text messages/objects from the survivor’s life.
- **Pacing:** leave a beat after the hindsight line. The pause should do more work than a dramatic music cue.
- **Palette and texture:** weathered but not gratuitously grim. Use practical light, muted colors, repaired objects, and evidence of adaptation.
- **Sound:** room tone, distant radio static, footsteps, appliances, and other ordinary sounds. Avoid action-movie impacts or triumphant music.
- **On-screen identifier:** give each narrator a first name or role and a year/location only when it helps the story. Do not overload the frame with fictional world-building.

## 6. Caption and CTA Pattern

Captions should state that the story is fictional while preserving the premise:

> An imagined survivor remembers the alert everyone ignored. Nuclear catastrophe does not need to be inevitable to be dangerous. We don't need nukes.

Suggested CTAs:

- “Share the story, then take the pledge.”
- “What ordinary warning would your town ignore?”
- “We should not leave survival to perfect decisions under impossible pressure.”
- “Add your voice: We Don't Need Nukes.”

Use platform-specific captions and alt text. Alt text should describe the visual and include the core message without relying on the meme’s text alone.

## 7. Boundaries and Review Rules

- Do not provide operational details about building, targeting, deploying, or evading nuclear weapons.
- Do not present invented events, locations, statistics, or survivor accounts as real testimony.
- Do not use real victims’ names, identifiable likenesses, or archival trauma without permission and clear context.
- Avoid graphic injury, mass-death imagery, spectacle, and jokes that make survivors the punchline.
- Keep the cause of the catastrophe intentionally broad unless a future, fact-checked episode requires more specificity.
- Review every asset for accidental partisan targeting, xenophobia, militaristic glamour, or language that treats any population as disposable.
- Label AI-generated visuals, voices, or scripts according to the platform’s rules and campaign policy. Do not imitate a real person’s voice or likeness.

## 8. First Production Batch

Produce an initial set of eight assets, one for each premise above:

- 4 direct-to-camera or voiceover videos, 30–60 seconds each.
- 4 static or carousel memes adapted from the same stories.
- One consistent end card with the campaign name and pledge URL.
- Captions, subtitles, alt text, and a fictional-story disclosure for every asset.

Test two opening lines and two closing lines per concept. Select follow-up stories based on completion rate, shares, thoughtful comments, and pledge clicks—not only raw views. The goal is recognition and conversation, not shock for its own sake.
